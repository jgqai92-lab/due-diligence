## Test Content (copy everything below this line into the Content field)

[Host]: Welcome back to Power & Pricing. Today we're diving into what I think is the single most underappreciated bottleneck in the AI buildout — not chips, not talent, but raw electrical power. My guest has been tracking utility-scale infrastructure for 15 years. Let's start with the demand picture.

[Guest]: The numbers are staggering. US data center power demand hit roughly 28 gigawatts in 2025 and we're projecting 45 to 50 gigawatts by 2028. That's nearly a doubling in three years. Goldman Sachs estimated in their May 2025 report that AI-driven electricity demand alone could add 200 terawatt-hours of incremental US consumption by 2030. To put that in perspective, that's equivalent to adding the entire electricity demand of a country like South Korea onto the US grid.

[Host]: And the hyperscalers are telegraphing this. Microsoft committed $80B in capex for FY2025, most of it going to AI data center infrastructure. Amazon Web Services announced $75B. Google committed $75B. Meta is spending $60B to $65B. Collectively that's roughly $290 billion in a single year — up from about $180 billion in 2024. That's a 61% year-over-year increase in aggregate hyperscaler capex.

[Guest]: Exactly. But here's the thesis: you can buy all the NVIDIA H100s and B200s you want, but if you can't plug them in, they're paperweights. The chip bottleneck is real, but it's a manufacturing problem — TSMC is adding capacity at their Arizona fab, Samsung is expanding in Texas, Intel Foundry is coming online. Those are 18 to 24 month problems. The energy bottleneck is structural and could persist through 2030 or beyond.

[Host]: Walk us through why.

[Guest]: Two fundamental constraints. First, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission — FERC — controls the interconnection queue for new generation and transmission projects. As of Q4 2025, there were over 2,600 gigawatts of generation capacity sitting in the interconnection queue, but FERC's average approval timeline has stretched to 5.1 years. They issued Order 2023 to try to reform the process, but implementation has been glacial. Only about 14% of projects that enter the queue actually reach commercial operation. That means the regulatory pipeline is a massive funnel with a tiny opening.

[Host]: And the second constraint?

[Guest]: Solar and battery storage. Everyone points to renewables as the answer, and they're right long-term, but the infrastructure required to make solar and battery tech applicable at scale for current and future AI demand is enormous. The US installed about 35 gigawatts of new solar in 2025 — a record — but we need 75 to 100 gigawatts annually by 2028 to keep pace with demand projections. That requires a tripling of installation capacity in three years. The supply chain isn't there yet. Polysilicon production is concentrated — over 80% comes from China. Domestic manufacturing under the IRA is ramping but First Solar's new Alabama facility won't reach full capacity until H2 2027.

[Guest]: On batteries, grid-scale storage deployments hit 18 gigawatt-hours in 2025, but we need something like 150 to 200 gigawatt-hours of annual deployment by 2030 to pair with intermittent solar and provide the 24/7 baseload that data centers require. That's a 10x gap. Companies like Fluence Energy ($FLNC), Tesla Energy, and EnerSys ($ENS) are scaling as fast as they can, but the lithium supply chain, transformer lead times of 2 to 3 years, and high-voltage switchgear shortages all constrain the pace.

[Host]: So who benefits from this bottleneck?

[Guest]: I see three tiers of beneficiaries. First, the natural gas bridge plays. If renewables can't scale fast enough, gas turbines fill the gap. GE Vernova ($GEV) has a massive backlog — they reported $44B in total backlog as of Q3 2025, with gas power orders up 35% YoY. Siemens Energy is in a similar position. Natural gas producers like EQT Corp ($EQT) — the largest US gas producer — benefit from structurally higher demand. Henry Hub gas prices have moved from $2.50 to around $4.20 per MMBtu over the past 18 months, and we think $5.00 is realistic by 2027.

[Guest]: Second tier is grid infrastructure. The transmission and distribution network needs $400 billion in investment through 2035 according to the DOE's National Transmission Planning Study. Companies like Quanta Services ($PWR), which does grid construction and modernization, posted $26B in backlog in Q4 2025 — a record. MasTec Inc ($MTZ) is another — they're building both renewable interconnections and gas pipeline infrastructure. On the equipment side, Eaton Corp ($ETN) is seeing 2 to 3 year lead times on medium-voltage switchgear and transformers. Their electrical Americas segment grew 22% in 2025.

[Guest]: Third tier is the nuclear renaissance. Microsoft signed a 20-year PPA with Constellation Energy ($CEG) to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1. Amazon has deals with Talen Energy ($TLN) for the Susquehanna nuclear plant. Small modular reactors from companies like NuScale Power ($SMR) and Oklo Inc ($OKLO) are targeting first commercial deployment by 2029 to 2030. The economics are compelling — nuclear provides 24/7 baseload at roughly $70 per megawatt-hour, versus $45 for solar plus $35 to $50 for battery storage needed to firm it up, totaling $80 to $95.

[Host]: What's the timeline for this thesis to play out?

[Guest]: The acute bottleneck phase is 2025 through 2028. That's when demand growth outpaces new supply most dramatically. We estimate a cumulative shortfall of 15 to 20 gigawatts of firm power capacity relative to data center demand by 2027. This forces hyperscalers into premium PPA pricing — we're seeing contracts signed at $90 to $120 per megawatt-hour in constrained markets like Northern Virginia, up from $50 to $60 just two years ago.

[Guest]: The investment implication is that energy infrastructure companies are in a multi-year earnings upgrade cycle. GE Vernova trades at roughly 28x forward earnings with 15% to 18% EPS growth expected through FY2028. Quanta Services trades at 22x with similar growth visibility. Eaton is at 30x but has the strongest margin expansion story — they're guiding to 25% EBITDA margins by 2027, up from 21% in 2024. On the gas side, EQT trades at only 8x forward earnings despite being the direct beneficiary of structurally higher gas prices.

[Host]: Risks?

[Guest]: Three main risks. First, demand disappointment — if AI adoption plateaus or hyperscaler spending gets cut, the whole thesis weakens. Second, faster-than-expected renewable deployment — a breakthrough in permitting reform or a technology step-change in battery density could accelerate the timeline. Third, political risk — changes to the IRA tax credits or FERC leadership turnover could alter the regulatory landscape. But the base case is strong: the energy gap is real, it's quantifiable, and the companies positioned to close it are seeing record order books.

[Host]: Great breakdown. For our listeners, the key takeaway is that while everyone's focused on chip supply, the binding constraint on AI infrastructure may actually be the electrical grid and power generation capacity — and that bottleneck creates a multi-year investment opportunity across natural gas, grid infrastructure, and nuclear. Thanks for joining us.
